# Analysis Report: The "Collocations" of 錢 in the 舊唐書·食貨志

This report uses collocation data of 錢 to analyze how currency was understood in Tang Dynasty China. Was money merely an important medium for commodity transactions and circulation? Or was it a prerogative held by the ruler? The text is selected from juan 48 to juan 49 of 舊唐書.（The Section on 食貨志）These two chapters cover the grain production at that time, along with the related systems, market rules, and financial operations and so on. The source of the text is Wikisource text (PD 945; CC BY-SA; 25% unproofread).

Use opencc to convert the entire text to simplified Chinese. I searched for terms related to money in the Tang Dynasty (開元通寶、惡錢、鑄錢、行用、見錢、鉛錫、兩稅) as a user dictionary, and put both into jieba for word segmentation. Next, set up a ±5 window that captures adjacent collocations, while a smaller window would be too restrictive and would exclude genuine collocations. The ±10 window captures collocations that reach across different parts of a sentence. The sentence level corresponds to the level of discourse and serves as the coarsest boundary. For filtering, I set min_word_length to 1, because in classical Chinese many content words are single characters, and a setting of 2 would miss them. I also set min_obs_local to 3 and used FDR-BH to correct for multiple testing, keeping only pairs whose adjusted p-value was 0.05 or lower. To understand what p ≤ 0.05 means, we first need to know what a contingency table does. You can think of it as a table of counts: it sorts the corpus into categories based on two things — whether the target word appears and whether the collocate appears. This yields four cells in all, and cell a holds the number of times the two words appear together. The point is this: the table does not merely count how often they appear together; it also counts the times they appear separately and never meet. If we looked only at the former, two very common words might simply happen to keep appearing together, and we would mistake them for a fixed collocation. So by counting the cases where they never meet as well, we can tell a genuine collocation apart from this kind of accidental overlap. Assuming that these two words have no connection with each other and appear independently, without any relationship between them. In that case, the p-value asks how likely it is that we would see the table in front of us, or an even more extreme one. When the p-value is very small, it means that if the two words really had no relation, such an extreme case of co-occurrence would be hard to explain by chance. And so the claim that the two are unrelated becomes harder and harder to hold. Next I compute and rank the collocations using both log-likelihood and logDice. Log-likelihood shows whether a collocation is significant: the less the co-occurrence looks like chance, the higher the score. LogDice shows how tightly the two words stick together, which stands for the strength of the link between them. So some words are very reliable without being tightly bound, while others only look paired because they are frequent. The two measures have to be read together for the conclusion to hold. These concepts are extremely important for us to acquire the appropriate collocations.

Once we gather the collocations of 钱, we can sort them into three groups. 铸、新、开元通宝、行用 form the first: they point to money coined by the state and to its power to set that money circulating. 旧、恶钱、铜、铅锡 form the second: they point to coins cut in weight and short on quality, which circulate beside old coins and throw money into disorder and decline. These are some paragraphs in the original text "盜鑄漸起", "惡錢轉多", "私鑄更多，錢復濫惡", "買錫熔銷" and "斯須則盈千百". Copper and lead-tin do not appear by chance. Again and again, the text puts bad coin next to the cheap metal it is made from. So once the window is widened, these two words show up beside 钱. 轻、重、一当、时价 form the third: they ask how much money is worth, how it is converted, and whether it matches the market price. Liu Zhi, a Tang official, argues that money should "平輕重而權本末", and he warns that "錢輕則傷賈". The court, meanwhile, tries to set the value of money by decree: it mints the Qianyuan coin at "一當十", and later at "一當五十". Prices then soar, until "米斗至七千，餓死者相枕于道". The pair of the official exchange rate and the market price captures exactly this clash: a value fixed by the state against a price set by the market. These were the issues that the officials of the Tang Dynasty were debating at that time — namely, how to set the value of currency. Together these collocations suggest that whether money holds, what it is worth, and whether it can circulate are not for the market to decide, but are set and kept by political authority. The chaos of bad coins and old coins circulating side by side proves the same point from the other side: monetary order rests on the authority of the state, and once that authority slips, the coinage falls apart. Tan Mei Ah, in "Monetary Policy as Key to State Authority and Income in Tang China" (Journal of Chinese Studies 64 [2017]: 35–109), reaches a congruent conclusion from the same 食貨志. She argues that Tang monetary policy "had two main priorities, beyond regulating price levels," namely "the preservation of state authority and the augmentation of state income," and that "the major objective in issuing full-bodied coins was to establish state authority" (37). On counterfeiting, too, she finds that "the major aims were again to reinstate sovereignty and also to withhold the rightful seignorage" (37).

The present results nonetheless have their limits. In classical Chinese, 钱 often appears only inside a longer word such as 铸钱、恶钱、见钱、两税钱, so how one counts it makes a difference. This project counts 钱 only where it stands on its own, which comes to 90 times. If the 钱 inside compounds is counted too, the character 錢 appears 275 times in all. The two counts differ by nearly a factor of three, and they change not only the total for 钱 but also which words end up as its collocates. Take 恶钱. Split it into 恶 and 钱, and 钱 is overcounted while 恶 becomes a collocate of its own. Keep 恶钱 as one word, and 钱 stays clean at 90, with 恶钱 itself appearing as a collocate. They also fall mostly in juan 48, which holds 231 of them against 44 in juan 49. The results therefore describe that one section rather than the whole 食貨志, and the money material in juan 49 （盐、茶、常平）, hardly enters the picture.
